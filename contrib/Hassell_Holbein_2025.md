# Potential Pitfalls in Difference-in-Differences Designs

**Contributor:** Haleen Jung

**Full citation:** Hassell, Hans J. G., and John B. Holbein. 2025. "Navigating Potential Pitfalls in Difference-in-Differences Designs: Reconciling Conflicting Findings on Mass Shootings' Effect on Electoral Outcomes." *American Political Science Review* 119(1): 240–260. doi:10.1017/S0003055424000108.

## 1. What the paper establishes

Difference-in-differences (DiD) estimates depend on a credible comparison of how treated and comparison units would have changed without treatment. Hassell and Holbein show how differential pretreatment trends, heterogeneous effects in two-way fixed-effects (TWFE) designs, and consequential specification choices can change the interpretation of those estimates.

Studies using DiD designs have reached conflicting conclusions about whether mass shootings increase the Democratic vote share in affected counties. The paper shows that counties experiencing shootings were already trending more Democratic than comparison counties; once this differential movement is addressed, the evidence no longer supports the previously claimed large electoral effect. They also examine whether a TWFE estimator mixes inappropriate comparisons when treatment timing and effects vary, but conclude that violations of parallel trends matter more in this application; across reasonable corrected specifications, small positive or negative effects remain possible.

## 2. Checks for a reviewer

**(a) Check whether a paper acknowledges potential issues with using DiD design.** If two assumptions -- parallel trends assumption and treatment effect homogeneity -- are violated, the result is likely misleading. 

**(b) Check the paper's evidence on pretreatment parallel trends.** Locate a comparison of treated and control outcomes before exposure, a placebo estimate using outcomes dated before treatment, or an event study with several pre-exposure periods. Ask whether the displayed periods, treatment definition, and comparison group match the headline analysis. Do not treat a nonsignificant lead as proof of parallel counterfactual trends.

**(c) If pretreatment trends differ, check what changes when the authors address that difference.** Compare the original estimate with justified unit-specific trends or another method suitable for time-varying confounding. Where plausible departures from exact parallel trends can be bounded, ask for a sensitivity analysis showing when the substantive conclusion ceases to hold.

**(d) If treatment begins at different times across units, check whether TWFE comparisons and changing treatment effects could distort the estimate.** Look for a decomposition of the simple TWFE result, when applicable, and a treatment-timing-aware estimate using appropriate comparison groups. Examine whether the alternative estimator also addresses any differential pretreatment trends.

**(e) Check whether the conclusion reflects the full set of substantively defensible specifications.** Compare effects under plausible treatment codings, exposure durations, comparison groups, and trend adjustments. Check whether the author(s) report the range of effect sizes and uncertainty, including estimates that change sign or lose significance, rather than foregrounding a single favorable coefficient.

## 3. Evidence

**For (a).** The authors write that studies finding a large positive effect on Democratic vote shares "are a product of a failure to properly specify difference-in-differences models when underlying assumptions are unlikely to hold" (p. 240). This supports checking whether a DiD analysis identifies and assesses threats to its causal assumptions.

**For (b).** The authors recommend checking for "impacts prior to treatment" (p. 247). Using outcomes from elections before shootings, their placebo models find an apparent shooting effect years before exposure; the event-study estimates likewise reveal an upward Democratic trajectory in shooting counties before the event (pp. 246–248, Figures 3–4). Figure 2 shows those counties already moving differently from counties without shootings (p. 245). These results make preperiod trends and placebo estimates concrete review targets. The authors caution that no single pretrend test can establish the unobserved parallel-trends assumption (p. 244).

**For (c).** The authors recommend "several parameterizations of the unit-specific trends" (p. 248), since the chosen functional form can influence the estimate. In their reanalysis, adding county trends greatly reduces the apparent post-shooting effect, and the authors distinguish that reduction in magnitude from merely losing precision (pp. 248–250, Figures 5–6). Their sensitivity analysis finds the positive conclusion vulnerable to "even minor parallel-trends violations" (p. 253, Figure 8). Together, these passages support checking both a defensible adjustment and how much violation the claim can withstand. However, the paper does not prescribe one trend form or sensitivity method for all designs (pp. 251–253).

**For (d).** The authors write that component TWFE "effects can be very different" (p. 254, Figure 9). With different treatment dates, the overall TWFE estimate combines two-group comparisons, some of which use units treated earlier as comparisons for units treated later (pp. 253–254). A method designed for effects that vary by treatment group or time since treatment substantially attenuates the long-run shooting estimates; adding county trends reduces them further (pp. 254–255, Figure 10). Note that the decomposition diagnoses simple TWFE and cannot decompose their trend-adjusted models (p. 254). A reviewer should therefore inspect the comparisons and a cleaner estimator without assuming that the latter alone solves the parallel-trends problem.

**For (e).** The authors favor a "preponderance of evidence" approach (p. 257). They distinguish coding only the election immediately after a shooting from coding every later election as treated, two choices with different implications for the duration of an effect (pp. 242–243, Figure 1). After correcting for differential trends, the estimates across reasonable specifications cluster near zero, sometimes change sign, and are only rarely statistically significant (pp. 256–257, Figure 11 panel c, d). This supports asking whether the paper's conclusion summarizes justified specifications and their uncertainty, including what large effects can be ruled out, rather than selecting one significant model.

## 4. Scope

**Applies to:** Check (a) applies when a paper relies on a difference-in-differences (DiD) design for its main causal claim. Check (b) applies when an observational DiD design has multiple untreated pretreatment periods and the treatment could not plausibly affect outcomes during those periods. Check (c) applies when treated and comparison units have different pretreatment slopes and the panel contains enough information to assess how those differences affect the estimate. Check (d) applies to staggered-adoption two-way fixed effects (TWFE) designs, especially when effects may differ across cohorts or with time since treatment. Check (e) applies when several substantively credible choices about exposure, follow-up, or adjustment could change the causal conclusion. These checks are especially relevant when treatment timing may be endogenous, adoption is staggered, and the panel contains many units relative to periods (p. 258).

**Does not directly apply to:** A design with very few treated units may require tools tailored to that setting, such as synthetic controls, rather than the full set of diagnostics used by Hassell and Holbein(2025) (p. 258). Likewise, the paper’s discussion of staggered TWFE does not directly govern an analysis whose treatment is randomly assigned and whose DiD estimate is used mainly to improve precision.

**Does not apply to:** The framework does not apply to a study that makes no causal claim from a DiD design. Check (d)’s concern about comparisons between already-treated and later-treated units does not arise in a simple two-group, two-period design with one treatment date and a never-treated comparison group. A Goodman–Bacon decomposition should not be requested for an estimate that the decomposition cannot represent: the authors limit its use to simple TWFE models, not their trend-adjusted estimates (p. 254).

**Partial application:** With only one observed pretreatment period, check (b) cannot require a multi-period event study. A period affected by credible anticipation is not an untreated placebo. Where pretreatment evidence is available, flat-looking trends or nonsignificant leads still cannot establish the counterfactual parallel-trends assumption; low-powered tests may miss consequential differences (pp. 244, 257). Under check (c), unit-specific trends are most interpretable when a smooth underlying trajectory is plausible, and their estimates concern deviations from that trajectory. A short or small panel does not justify a routine demand for high-order trends or one fixed sensitivity package (pp. 248, 251–253). Under check (d), treatment-effect heterogeneity warrants investigation, but it does not make every TWFE result invalid; in this application, differential pretreatment trends mattered more (pp. 254–255, 257). Under check (e), reviewers should compare credible alternative specifications, including immediate and persistent treatment coding when both are plausible, while recognizing that they answer different questions (pp. 242–243). Across these checks, a nonsignificant estimate should be interpreted alongside the range of effects its confidence interval can exclude (pp. 242, 248–249, 257).

## 5. What failure looks like

A paper presents a DiD estimate as its main causal result but treats its TWFE specification as sufficient without assessing threats to the identifying assumptions. When multiple pretreatment periods are available, its figures omit them or the text treats nonsignificant leads as proof of parallel trends. If the displayed pretreatment slopes differ, the paper offers neither a justified adjustment nor a sensitivity analysis, and its conclusion does not discuss how those checks affect the estimate. In different-time groups where effects may vary, it gives no account of the comparisons underlying its simple TWFE estimate or results from a suitable time-group-aware estimator. 

## 6. Test cases

**SHOULD trigger:** Lyons, Jeffrey, and Josh M. Ryan. 2026. “Lights, Camera, Inaction? The Effects of Gavel-to-Gavel Floor Coverage on U.S. State Legislatures.” *American Political Science Review* 120(2): 641–661. State chambers adopted floor coverage in different years, and the paper’s primary analysis uses TWFE DiD (p. 648), so checks (a), (b), (d), and (e) should run. The authors already discuss the assumptions and report pre-outcome tests, cohort analyses, a Goodman–Bacon decomposition, and a cleaner-treatment subsample (pp. 645, 649–651). A reviewer should assess those results rather than claim they are absent; check (c) requires evidence of differing pretreatment slopes, not staggered adoption alone.

**Should NOT trigger:** Hayes, Matthew, David Fortunato, and Matthew V. Hibbing. 2021. “Race–Gender Bias in White Americans’ Preferences for Gun Availability.” *Journal of Public Policy* 41(4): 818–834. The study randomly assigns race and gender cues in a survey and compares respondents’ gun-availability preferences (pp. 823–824). It has no before-and-after treated–comparison design or DiD-based causal claim, so checks (a)–(e) should remain silent despite the shared gun-policy topic.
